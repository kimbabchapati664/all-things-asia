For Korean adoptees searching

Over 200,000 Korean children were sent abroad for adoption since the 1950s. If you are one of them and you have started wondering about the beginning of your story, this site collects the real, verified places to start: free DNA registration, your official records, the associations built by adoptees, and what it takes to come back.

The order that works

Five steps, in the order most people should take them

Each step is free or nearly free. You can do the first three without leaving your country.

  1. File an Adoption Information Disclosure Request

    Everything else depends on this. Since July 2025 all Korean adoption records are held centrally by the National Center for the Rights of the Child (NCRC), not by the agencies. You must file it yourself, and it is free. How to file →

  2. Register your DNA with the Korean police — free

    If your file has no usable birth-parent information, NCRC issues a certificate that lets you give a DNA sample at a Korean embassy or consulate in your own country. It goes into the national missing-persons database. 35 missions in 14 countries do this. Where to register →

  3. Test with 325KAMRA and mirror your results

    An adoptee-run nonprofit sends free FamilyTreeDNA kits worldwide for a $10 admin fee. Then upload your raw data to Ancestry, MyHeritage and GEDmatch so a searching relative can find you in any database. Get a kit →

  4. Join an adoptee-led organisation

    GOA'L in Seoul, KoRoot, Adoptee Hub, and your own country's association. They do the 1:1 search work, the interpreting, and the part nobody warns you about: what happens after a match. Find your people →

  5. Plan the trip — visa, records visit, language

    An F-4 visa lets you stay longer than 90 days. You can book an in-person file review at NCRC in Seoul or the archive in Goyang. Nationality restoration is possible too. Coming to Korea →

All Things Asia

I was born in Busan. I have lived in Madagascar, France, India and Singapore, and then I came home. I speak Korean, French, English and Hindi.

I accompany Korean adoptees who come back to search: sitting with them at agency and government offices, interpreting the conversations that matter most, and staying in the room afterwards. This site is the reference list I kept wishing existed, written in plain English and kept up to date.
