Section 1

Where to register your DNA

Start with the free government registration. Then widen the net with commercial databases, because a match only happens if you and your relative are in the same one.

Important order of operations. The free Korean police DNA registration is only opened to you after you file an Adoption Information Disclosure Request and NCRC confirms your file has no usable birth-parent information. So file the records request first (Section 3), even if DNA is your real goal.
Korean police DNA registration for adoptees 무연고 입양인 유전자 등록 · NCRC 아동권리보장원 + 경찰청

The official route, and the only one connected to Korea's national missing-persons DNA database, where searching birth families give their samples. You do not have to travel to Korea: since 2020 you can give your sample at a Korean embassy or consulate, currently 35 diplomatic missions across 14 countries. NCRC first issues a "Certificate of Adoptee Without Identifying Birth Parent Information for Genetic Testing," then your local mission collects the sample and sends it to the Korean National Police Agency.

How to start: email your signed application, consent form and a copy of your ID to familysearch@ncrc.or.kr, then contact your nearest Korean embassy or consulate. NCRC family-search line: +82-2-6454-8500, weekdays 09:00–16:00 KST.

325KAMRA Korean Adoptees Making Reunions Achievable · adoptee-founded, 2015

A nonprofit run by adoptees that mails free FamilyTreeDNA kits worldwide, and also collects DNA from birth families inside Korea, which is the part almost nobody else does. They have volunteer "search angels" who build family trees from your matches, and they help with first contact and translation in Korea after a match. They report 422 notifications of immediate-family DNA matches since 2016.

Cost: the kit itself is free; there is a non-refundable $10 USD administrative fee, and you pay return postage and any customs charges. Kits generally ship at the start of each month. Already tested elsewhere? You can transfer your raw data into FamilyTreeDNA and join their project group instead.

Commercial DNA databasesFamilyTreeDNA, Ancestry, MyHeritage, GEDmatch, 23andMe

Test once, then upload your raw data everywhere you can. FamilyTreeDNA matters most because 325KAMRA uses it, so Korean birth-family samples land there. GEDmatch and MyHeritage accept free uploads of raw data from other companies. Ancestry has the largest overall database.

An honest warning: DNA testing is not common in South Korea. Whether you get a close match usually depends on whether someone in your birth family was also testing. Adoptees with an American birth father tend to find far more matches, simply because testing rates in the United States are much higher.

Section 2

Korean adoptee associations

The groups in Korea do the search work on the ground. The groups in your own country are the ones who will understand you at 2am.

In Korea

G.O.A.'L — Global Overseas Adoptees' Link해외입양인연대 · Seoul, founded 1998

The first adoptee-led NGO in Korea, and the most practically useful single organisation for an active search. They do 1:1 search consultations, review your file with you, run search itineraries inside Korea, interpret before and after reunion, and guide F-4 visa and dual citizenship applications. They also support adoptees in crisis and deported adoptees.

Their adoption agency directory is the best cross-reference anywhere: it maps foreign agencies (Holt International, WACAP, Bethany, DanAdopt/DIA, Adoptionscentrum, Wereldkinderen, Rayon de Soleil, Spence-Chapin and others) to the Korean agency that actually holds your file.

Cost: most individual services require membership. +82-2-325-6585 · gcr@goal.or.kr

KoRoot뿌리의집 · Jongno-gu, Seoul, founded 2003

Runs a guesthouse for returning adoptees, plus family tracing, legal support, trauma counselling and cultural programs. KoRoot is also the most important political node in the truth-commission cases: it represents adoptee rights groups from Australia, the United States and Sweden before Korea's Truth and Reconciliation Commission, and works with sister groups in Denmark, Norway, the Netherlands, Belgium and the francophone countries.

Section 3

Birth family search and your official records

This is the door everything else opens from. It is free, and you have to walk through it yourself.

What changed in 2025. On 19 July 2025 Korea's public adoption system took effect and all adoption records were centralised at NCRC, not the agencies. About 260,000 volumes of files were physically moved into an archive in Goyang, Gyeonggi-do. NCRC's own guidance: send all record enquiries to NCRC, not to your original adoption agency. In October 2025 Korea also ratified the Hague Convention on intercountry adoption.
Adoption Information Disclosure Request입양정보공개청구 · NCRC

You must file this yourself. If you are under 18 you need your adoptive parents' consent. Adoptees processed under the Civil Act rather than the special adoption laws cannot use this route.

What you get without anyone's permission: background information — adoption date, the stated reason for relinquishment, your pre-adoption name, your date and time of birth, and your birth parents' ages at the time.

What needs consent: your birth parents' identifying information — name, date of birth, address, contact. It is released only if they consent. If consent is not given, GOA'L advises waiting a year and requesting again.

Reviewing your file in personSeoul office or the Goyang archive

Once your disclosure request is filed, you can book an in-person file review, either at NCRC's Seoul office or at the temporary archive facility in Deogyang-gu, Goyang. Reservations open 14 to 50 days ahead, and slots are limited — NCRC raised its daily in-person consultations from 3 to 5 for October 2026 because of the IKAA Gathering. A disclosure request must already be on file before you can book.

The four Korean adoption agencies

Records are now held by NCRC, but agency names still matter for identifying your paperwork. Two pieces of advice from GOA'L: contact the Korean agency, not your agency in the adoptive country, because foreign agencies often charge high fees for file requests that are free from the Korean side. And if you were privately adopted with no agency involved, go straight to NCRC.

Two things to know about NCRC. First, NCRC has warned that private homeland-visit programs are not affiliated with it, and that donation solicitations by such programs are unrelated to NCRC. Second, NCRC suffered a data breach between 30 April and 2 May 2026 that exposed sensitive adoptee records; affected people were notified on 3 May 2026, and adoptee groups have publicly questioned the agency's handling of it. That does not change the fact that NCRC is the only holder of your file, but you deserve to know.

Visiting and living in Korea

Staying longer than 90 days, getting your nationality back, and what support exists if Korea becomes home again.

F-4 visa (재외동포)

A multiple-entry visa and residence card for people of Korean descent who want to stay longer than three months. GOA'L guides adoptees through the application; NCRC issues the Adoption Verification Certificate the application needs.

GOA'L F-4 guide →

Nationality restoration / dual citizenship

Adoptees can restore Korean nationality and hold dual citizenship. The required Adoption Verification Certificate comes from NCRC, which improved its issuance process in August 2025.

GOA'L citizenship guide →

Korean language and scholarships

GOA'L runs Korean language scholarships for adoptee members. InKAS has also historically funded language schools.

Adoptee Hub publishes "Living in Korea: A Guide by Adoptees," written by people who did it. KoRoot's guesthouse in Jongno is the usual soft landing for a first long stay.

If things go wrong

There is integrated support for adoptees in crisis and for deported adoptees. NCRC: +82-2-6454-8645 / 8646. The delegated operating agency is GOA'L: +82-2-325-6585.

US citizenship gap

Thousands of intercountry adoptees were never naturalised by their adoptive parents and some have been deported. Adoptees For Justice campaigns for the PAAF Act to fix it retroactively.

Section 5

Homeland trips and coming back

Group programs, and how to tell a good one from one that is selling you something free.

Read this before you pay anyone. Your records request, your file review and your DNA registration are all free and are handled by NCRC and the police. NCRC has publicly stated that private homeland-visit programs are not affiliated with it and that their donation solicitations are unrelated. A good program is honest that it is selling logistics, company and interpretation — not access.
